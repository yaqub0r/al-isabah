#### English

1805—Hakim ibn Hizam [1]

[1] Asad al-Ghaba, biog. no. 1234; al-Isti'ab, biog. no. 553; Musnad Ahmad, 4/401, 403; Nasab Quraysh, 231; Tabaqat Khalifa, biog. nos. 70, 473; Tarikh al-Bukhari, 3/11; al-Ma'arif, 311; al-Jarh wa-al-Ta'dil, 3/202; al-Mustadrak, 3/482–485; Jamharat Ansab al-Arab, 121; al-Jam' Bayt Rijal al-Sahihayn, 1/105; Tarikh Ibn Asakir, 5/123; Tahdhib al-Asma wa-al-Lughat, q. 1, h. 1, 166; Tahdhib al-Kamal, 321; Tarikh al-Islam, 2/277; al-Ibar, 1/60; Tadhhib al-Tahdhib, 1/169; Mirat al-Janan, 1/127.

He was Hakim ibn Hizam ibn Khuwaylid ibn Asad ibn Abd al-Uzza ibn Qusayy, al-Asadi, the son of Khadija’s brother, Khadija being the wife of the Prophet, may God bless him and grant him peace.

His mother’s name was Safiyya; it is also said to have been Fakhita; and it is also said that she was Zaynab bint Zuhayr ibn al-Harith ibn Asad ibn Abd al-Uzza. His kunya was Abu Khalid. He has hadiths in the Six Books.

His son Hizam, Abd Allah ibn al-Harith ibn Nawfal, Sa'id ibn al-Musayyib, Musa ibn Talha, Urwa, and others related from him.

Musa ibn Uqba said, on the authority of Abu Habiba, the client of al-Zubayr: I heard Hakim ibn Hizam say:

“I was born thirteen years before the Year of the Elephant, and I was old enough to understand when Abd al-Muttalib intended to sacrifice his son Abd Allah.”

Al-Waqidi related something similar and added that this was five years before the birth of the Prophet, may God bless him and grant him peace.

Hakim’s father was killed in the Fijar War, and Hakim took part in it.

Al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar related that Hakim was born inside the Kaaba. He said: He was one of the leading men of Quraysh and was a friend of the Prophet, may God bless him and grant him peace, before the prophetic mission. He remained fond of him and loved him after the mission, but delayed accepting Islam until he became Muslim in the Year of the Conquest.

It is established in the Sira and in the Sahih that he, may God bless him and grant him peace, said: “Whoever enters the house of Hakim ibn Hizam is safe.”

He was among those whose hearts were reconciled. He was present at Hunayn and was given one hundred camels from its spoils. Thereafter, his adherence to Islam became excellent. He had been present at Badr with the unbelievers and escaped with those who escaped. Whenever he sought to make a solemn oath, he would say: “By Him who saved me on the day of Badr.” His kunya was Abu Khalid.

Al-Zubayr said: Islam came while the rifada—the office of provisioning the pilgrims—was in Hakim’s hands. He used to do good and maintain ties of kinship.

It is related in the Sahih that he asked the Prophet, may God bless him and grant him peace: “There are things I used to do during the Jahiliyya; is there any reward for me in them?” He replied: “You embraced Islam with the good you had previously done credited to you.” [1]

[1] Reported by Ahmad in al-Musnad, 3/402; al-Bayhaqi in al-Sunan al-Kubra, 9/123 and 10/316; al-Tabarani in al-Kabir, 3/210, 213; Kanz al-Ummal, hadith no. 1341; al-Bukhari in al-Tarikh al-Saghir, 70; and Muslim, 1/113–114, in the Book of Faith, chapter 55, “Clarification of the ruling concerning an unbeliever’s deeds,” hadith nos. 194 and 195.

Dar al-Nadwa was in his possession, and he later sold it to Muawiya for one hundred thousand dirhams. Ibn al-Zubayr reproached him, but he said to him: “My brother’s son, I have purchased with it a house in Paradise.” He then gave all the dirhams in charity. He was among those knowledgeable about the genealogies and historical reports of Quraysh.

He died in the year 50; it is also said that he died in 54, in 58, or in 60. He was among those who lived for one hundred and twenty years, half of them in the Jahiliyya and half in Islam.

Al-Bukhari said in al-Tarikh: He died in the year 60 at the age of one hundred and twenty. Ibrahim ibn al-Mundhir said this. Al-Bukhari then transmitted through Umar ibn Abd Allah ibn Urwa, from Urwa, who said: “He died ten years into the caliphate of Muawiya.”

#### English

2263—Khuzayma ibn Hakim al-Sulami «4»: al-Bahzi. He is also said to be Ibn Thabit.

Ibn Shahin and others mentioned him, and Ibn Manda stated that he was related by marriage to Khadija, Mother of the Believers.

Ibn Mardawayh narrated in al-Tafsir, by way of Abu Imran al-Jawni, from Ibn Jurayj, from Ata, from Jabir, that Khuzayma ibn Thabit—not the Ansari—asked the Prophet ﷺ about “the secure city,” and he replied, “Mecca.”

Al-Tabarani narrated it in al-Awsat through this same route in a very lengthy form. It begins by stating that he was in a caravan belonging to Khadija with the Prophet ﷺ, and he said to him, “O Muhammad, I see qualities in you, and I bear witness that you are the Prophet who will arise in Tihama. I have believed in you, and when I hear that you have come forth, I shall come to you.” He delayed coming to the Prophet ﷺ until the Day of the Conquest, when he came to him. When he saw him, he said, “Welcome to the first emigrant . . .” The hadith.

He said, “No one narrated it from Ibn Jurayj except Abu Imran.”

Abu Musa said, “Abu Mashar and Ubayd ibn Hakim narrated it from Ibn Jurayj, from al-Zuhri, as a mursal report; however, they called him Khuzayma ibn Hakim al-Sulami.”

Ibn Shahin likewise named him thus, by way of Yazid ibn Iyad, from al-Zuhri, who said, “Khuzayma ibn Hakam used to visit Khadija every year, and there was kinship between them. He came to her, and she sent him with the Prophet ﷺ . . .” He related it at length over two folios. It contains much that is unusual, and its isnad is extremely weak, in addition to being interrupted.

We also transmitted it in Ibn Asakir’s Tarikh by way of Ubayd ibn Hakim, from Ibn Khurayj, at similar length.

#### English

2847 Z—Zuhayr ibn Qunfudh al-Asadi:

Al-Fakihi mentioned him in Akhbar Makka, by way of Zakariyya, by way of Zakariyya ibn Qatan (2), from Safiyya bint Zuhayr ibn Qunfudh al-Asadiyya, from her father, that the Prophet—may God bless him and grant him peace—would stay at Hira during the day; when night came, he would descend from Hira. He would then go to the mosque in the mountain pass, and Khadija would come to him from Mecca and meet him at the mosque in the mountain pass. When dawn drew near, they would part.

His lineage was given above in the entry on his son, Usama ibn Zayd. Ibn Sa‘d said: His mother was Su‘da bint Tha‘laba ibn Amir, of Banu Ma‘n of Tayy. I say: Al-Waqidi mentioned it decisively through an isnad of his from Sulayman ibn Yasar. Za’ida also stated it.

Zayd ibn Haritha witnessed Badr and the campaigns thereafter. He was killed in the expedition of Mu’ta while serving as commander. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, also left him as his deputy over Medina on some of his journeys.

It is related from al-Bara ibn Azib that Zayd ibn Haritha said, “O Messenger of Allah, you established a bond of brotherhood between me and Hamza.” Abu Ya‘la transmitted it.

It is related from Aisha: “The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, never sent Zayd ibn Haritha on a detachment without appointing him commander over its men. Had he survived, he would have appointed him his successor.” Abu Bakr ibn Abi Shayba transmitted it from her with a strong isnad.

Salama ibn al-Akwa said: “I campaigned with the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, in seven campaigns, and with Zayd ibn Haritha in seven campaigns, in which the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, appointed him commander over us.” Al-Bukhari transmitted it.

Al-Waqidi said: The first of Zayd’s detachments was to al-Qarada (1), then to al-Jumum (2), then to al-‘Is (3), then to al-Taraf (4), then to Hisma, then to Umm Qirfa, and then came his appointment as commander of the expedition of Mu’ta. He was martyred there at the age of fifty-five. By agreement, no one is named by his personal name in the Quran except him—and then al-Sijill, if that is established.

It is related from Muhammad ibn Usama ibn Zayd, from his father, that the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said to Zayd ibn Haritha: “O Zayd, you are my mawla; you are of me and belong to me, and you are the person dearest to me.” Ibn Sa‘d transmitted it with a hasan isnad. It is also found in Ahmad in a longer form.

Ibn Umar related that the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: “By Allah, he was indeed fit for command”—meaning Zayd ibn Haritha—“and he was indeed among the people dearest to me.” Al-Bukhari transmitted it.

Al-Tirmidhi and others related from the hadith of Aisha, who said: “Zayd ibn Haritha arrived in Medina while the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, was in my house. He came to him and knocked at the door, whereupon he rose to meet him, embraced him, and kissed him.”

It is related from Ibn Umar: “Umar assigned Usama a larger stipend than he assigned me. I asked him about it, and he replied: ‘He was dearer to the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, than you were, and his father was dearer to the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, than your father was.’” Authentic.

(4) Al-Taraf: pronounced Taraf, with a-vowels, and ending in fa. 4321 Z—al-Tahir:

Son of the master of creation, Muhammad ibn Abdullah ibn Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim. His mother was Khadija bint Khuwaylid.

Al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar said in the biography of Khadija in the Book of Genealogy: “My cousin Musab related to me, saying: Khadija bore the Prophet—may Allah bless him and grant him peace—al-Qasim and al-Tahir, who was also called al-Tayyib. Likewise, Yazid ibn Iyad, from al-Zuhri, mentioned only al-Qasim and Abdullah.

Al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar transmitted it from Muhammad ibn Hasan, from Muhammad ibn Fulayh, from him. (2)

Al-Zubayr said: “Ibrahim ibn Hamza also related to me, saying: Khadija bore al-Qasim and al-Tahir—and they say Abdullah and al-Tayyib”; and he mentioned the daughters.

He was his firstborn and the first child born to him, and after him he took his kunya.

8695—Nabbash ibn Zarara (1)

Ibn Mandah said: “He is mentioned in the accounts of the military campaigns. He accompanied the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him).” Thus he mentioned him, in brief.

Abu Musa said: “Nabbash ibn Zarara al-Tamimi, Abu Hala: al-Mustaghfiri included him among the Companions in the chapter on the letter nun.” Ibn al-Athir objected to this and set forth his genealogy, saying: “[Nabbash] ibn Zarara ibn Waqdan ibn Habib ibn Salama ibn Adi ibn Jurwa ibn Usayyid ibn Amr ibn Tamim, Abu Hala al-Tamimi.” He then said: “Musab al-Zubayri said: ‘He was a confederate of Banu Abd al-Dar.’” Ibn al-Athir said: “Abu Musa added him as a supplement to Ibn Mandah, although Ibn Mandah had already mentioned him, so there was no reason for this supplementary inclusion. Neither Zarara nor his son was a Companion.” End quote.

As for his objection to Abu Musa, it is apposite, since Abu Musa gave Nabbash the kunya Abu Hala and said that he was a Tamimi. His objection concerning Ibn Mandah, however, is open to question, because Ibn Mandah did not set forth the man’s genealogy, so it was possible that he was another person. It was for this reason that Abu Musa added him as a supplement, tracing the report by isnad to al-Mustaghfiri’s mention of him. Al-Mustaghfiri’s basis for mentioning him was the report he transmitted through Musab al-Zubayri, who said: “Nabbash ibn Zarara al-Tamimi, Abu Hala, a confederate of Banu Abd al-Dar, was the father of Hind, the son of Khadija.” End quote, abridged.

There is nothing in this that indicates he was a Companion, because Musab was discussing genealogies simply as genealogies, not specifically from the standpoint of the Companions.

8912 — Nastur the Monk

Ibn Sa'd related from al-Waqidi that, before the prophetic mission and before the Prophet (may God bless him and grant him peace) married Khadija, she entered into an agreement with him concerning a trading journey to al-Sham and sent her servant Maysara with him. 8935 — Hala ibn Abi Hala al-Tamimi (1)

Abu Umar said: He had Companionship. Ibn Hibban said: Hala ibn Khadija, the wife of the Prophet ﷺ, had Companionship. The name of Abu Hala was Hind ibn al-Nabbash ibn Zurara ibn Waqdan ibn Habib ibn Salama ibn Ghudhayy ibn Jarda ibn Usayyid—the latter in the diminutive form, with gemination—ibn Amr ibn Tamim.

In this genealogy, Ibn Makula vocalized Ghudhayy in the diminutive form, and related that al-Zubayr mentioned it in the usual, non-diminutive form. The correct form is the diminutive.

Al-Tabarani transmitted from Ali ibn Muhammad ibn Amr ibn Tamim, from Zayd ibn Hala ibn Abi Hala al-Tamimi in Egypt: “My father related to me, from his father Tamim, from his father Zayd ibn Hala, from his father Hala ibn Abi Hala, that he entered upon the Prophet ﷺ while he was asleep. He awoke, drew Hala to his breast, and said, ‘Hala! Hala! Hala!’”

Jafar al-Mustaghfiri transmitted, by way of Mu’ammal ibn Ismail, from Hammad ibn Salama, from Hisham, from his father, from Aisha. He said: “A son of Khadija called Hala arrived while the Prophet ﷺ was taking his midday rest. During his rest, he heard ‘Hala,’ awoke, and said, ‘Hala! Hala!’”

Jafar said: Musa ibn Ismail differed from him and related from Hammad, with this isnad: “He said: Hala was Khadija’s sister.” Jafar said: “This is the correct version.” End quote.

Hala, Khadija’s sister, has indeed been mentioned by way of Ali ibn Mushir, from Hisham, from his father, from Aisha, in the Sahih.

9027—Hind ibn Abi Hala al-Tamimi [1]

The stepson of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him). His mother was Khadija, the wife of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him).

He narrated from the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him). Al-Hasan ibn Ali narrated from him the description of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him). Al-Tirmidhi, al-Baghawi, al-Tabarani, and others transmitted it through several routes from al-Hasan ibn Ali. It also reached us with a high chain in the Mashyakha of Abu Ali ibn Shadhan, through the route of the Ahl al-Bayt.

Al-Baghawi also transmitted it, and Ibn Manda transmitted it by way of Yaqub al-Taymi, from Ibn Abbas, who said to Hind ibn Abi Hala, “Describe the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) to me.” Al-Baghawi said, from his uncle, from Abu Ubayd: “The name of Abu Hala, Khadija’s husband before the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him), was al-Nabbash ibn Zarara, and his son was Hind ibn al-Nabbash ibn Zarara ibn Waqdan ibn Habib ibn Salama ibn Ghadi ibn Jurda ibn Usayd ibn Amr ibn Tamim, a confederate of the Banu Abd al-Dar.” It is also said that he was Zarara ibn al-Nabbash.

Al-Zubayr said: “His name was Malik ibn al-Nabbash ibn Zarara.” Abu Muhammad ibn Hazm said: “Abu Hala’s name was Hind ibn Zarara ibn al-Nabbash.” I found that he had a predecessor in this view. Ibn Abi Khaythama said: “Ahmad ibn al-Miqdam narrated to us; Zuhayr ibn al-Ala narrated to us; Said narrated to us; Qatada said: ‘Abu Hala was Hind ibn Zarara ibn al-Nabbash.’” I saw in al-Marzubani’s Mu'jam al-Shu'ara that Zarara ibn al-Nabbash composed an elegy for the unbelievers slain at Badr, but he did not mention that Zarara was Muslim.

Ibn al-Sakan and Ibn Qani transmitted, by way of Sayf ibn Umar, from Abd Allah ibn Muhammad, from Hind ibn Hind ibn Abi Hala, from his father, who said: “I said, ‘O Messenger of Allah, what caused you to separate your daughter from Utayba’—meaning Ibn Abi Lahab—‘thereby provoking him against you?’ He replied, ‘Allah has refused to permit me to marry, or to give anyone in marriage, except to the people of Paradise.’”

Al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar said: “Hind was killed alongside Ali on the Day of the Camel.” Al-Daraqutni likewise said this in Kitab al-Ikhwa. Abu Umar said: “He was eloquent and articulate. He described the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him), and did so excellently and precisely.”

9151—Waraqa ibn Nawfal ibn Asad [4]

Ibn Abd al-Uzza ibn Qusayy, al-Qurashi al-Asadi, the paternal cousin of Khadija, wife of the Prophet (may Allah bless him and grant him peace).

Al-Tabari, al-Baghawi, Ibn Qani, Ibn al-Sakan, and others included him among the Companions. All of them cited, by way of Rawh ibn Musafir—one of the weak transmitters—from al-A'mash, from Abd Allah ibn Abd Allah, from:

(1) Usd al-Ghaba, entry 5462.

(2) Usd al-Ghaba, entry 5460.

(3) Usd al-Ghaba, entry 5464.

(4) Usd al-Ghaba, entry 5465.

Sa'id ibn Jubayr, from Ibn Abbas, from Waraqa ibn Nawfal, who said: I said, “O Muhammad, how does the one who comes to you come to you?” He replied: “He comes to me from heaven; his two wings are pearl, and the undersides of his feet are green.” [1]

Ibn Asakir said: “Ibn Abbas did not hear from Waraqa, and I know of no one who said that he embraced Islam.”

Al-Tabari distinguished the subject of this hadith from Waraqa ibn Nawfal al-Asadi. The story, however, differs from the story of Waraqa found in the two Sahihs, by way of al-Zuhri, from Urwa, from Aisha: “The first thing with which the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and grant him peace) was begun…”—the hadith concerning Gabriel's coming at Hira. It contains:

“Khadija then took him to Waraqa ibn Nawfal ibn Asad ibn Abd al-Uzza, Khadija's paternal cousin. He had become a Christian during the pre-Islamic period…”—the hadith.

It also contains: “Waraqa said, ‘This is the Namus [2] who was sent down to Moses. Would that I were a vigorous young man then! Would that I might be alive when your people drive you out.’” At its end it says: “It was not long before Waraqa died.” Its apparent meaning is that he acknowledged his prophethood. He died before—“Bahira” occurs here in the canonical Arabic, leaving the syntax unresolved—the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and grant him peace) called the people to Islam, and so he would be like Bahira.

There is cause for scrutiny in affirming Companionship for him. Nevertheless, in the additions to al-Maghazi, in the transmission of Yunus ibn Bukayr from Ibn Ishaq, Yunus ibn Bukayr said, from Yunus ibn Amr—namely, Ibn Abi Ishaq al-Sabi'i—from his father, from his grandfather, from Abu Maysara, whose name was Amr ibn Shurahbil and who was one of the senior Successors: The Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and grant him peace) said to Khadija, “When I am alone in seclusion, I hear a call, and, by Allah, I have become afraid for myself.” She replied, “Allah forbid! Allah would never do such a thing to you. By Allah, you faithfully discharge trusts…”—the hadith.

Waraqa said to him: “Receive glad tidings, and again receive glad tidings! I testify that you are the one whose coming the Son of Mary announced; that you follow a Namus like the Namus of Moses; that you are a prophet sent by Allah; and that, after this day of yours, you will assuredly be commanded to wage jihad. If I live to see that, I shall certainly fight alongside you.” When Waraqa died, the Messenger of Allah (may Allah bless him and grant him peace) said: “I saw the priest in Paradise, wearing garments of silk, because he believed in me and affirmed my truthfulness.” [3]

10182—Abu al-As ibn al-Rabi

Ibn Abd al-Uzza (1) ibn Abd Shams ibn Abd Manaf, al-Abshami. His mother was Hala bint Khuwaylid, and he was nicknamed Jarw al-Batha (“the Whelp of al-Batha”).

(1) Durr al-Sahaba, 782; al-Tabaqat al-Kubra, 2/18, 8/30.

Al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar said: He was called al-Amin. There was disagreement concerning his name. It was said to be Laqit—this was stated by Musab al-Zubayri, Amr ibn Ali al-Fallas, al-Alai, al-Hakim Abu Ahmad, and others, and al-Baladhuri considered it the preferable view. It was also said to be al-Zubayr; al-Zubayr related this from Uthman ibn al-Dahhak. It was said to be Hushaym, as related by Ibn Abd al-Barr. It was also said to be Mihsham—with the first letter bearing kasra, the second quiescent, and the dotted shin bearing fatha; and it was said to be Muhashshim—with the first letter bearing damma, the second fatha, and the doubled shin kasra—as related by al-Zubayr and al-Baghawi.

Ibn Manda related—and Abu Nuaym followed him—that it was said that his name was Yasir, but I think this is a corruption of Yasim.

Before the prophetic mission, according to what al-Zubayr related from his uncle Musab—and as some learned people asserted—he was bound in brotherhood with the Messenger of God ﷺ and frequently visited him at his home. The Messenger married him to his daughter Zaynab, the eldest of his daughters, who was the daughter of Abu al-As’s maternal aunt Khadija. It nevertheless happened that he did not accept Islam until after the Hijra.

Ibn Ishaq said: He was one of the men of Mecca renowned for wealth, trustworthiness, and commerce.

Al-Hakim Abu Ahmad transmitted with a sound chain from al-Shabi, who said: Zaynab, daughter of the Messenger of God ﷺ, was married to Abu al-As ibn al-Rabi. She emigrated while Abu al-As remained upon his religion. It happened that he set out for Syria on a trading journey. When he was near Medina, some Muslims wanted to go out against him, seize what he had, and kill him. News of this reached Zaynab, and she said, “O Messenger of God, are not the Muslims’ compact and covenant one?”

He said, “Yes.” She said, “Then bear witness that I have granted protection to Abu al-As.” When the Companions of the Messenger of God ﷺ saw this, they went out to him unarmed, without weapons. They said to him, “O Abu al-As, you are a man of high standing among Quraysh, and you are the cousin and son-in-law of the Messenger of God ﷺ. Would you accept Islam and thereby gain possession of the property of the people of Mecca that is with you?” He said, “Evil indeed is what you have instructed me to do: to renounce my religion through an act of treachery.” He continued until he reached Mecca and delivered to every person entitled to something his due. Then—al-Shabi said—he said, “O people of Mecca, have I discharged my obligation?” They said, “By God, yes.” He then said, “I bear witness that there is no deity but God and that Muhammad is the Messenger of God.” He then came to Medina as an emigrant, and the Messenger of God ﷺ restored his wife to him under the original marriage contract.

Notwithstanding the soundness of its chain as far as al-Shabi, this is a mursal report, and it is anomalous, being contradicted by a report more firmly established than it. In Ibn Ishaq’s al-Maghazi there is: Yahya ibn Abbad ibn Abd Allah ibn al-Zubayr related to me, from his father, from Aisha, who said: When the people of Mecca sent ransoms for their captives, Zaynab, daughter of the Messenger of God ﷺ, sent a necklace of hers that Khadija had sent her in wearing when she entered upon Abu al-As. When the Messenger of God ﷺ saw it, he felt intense tenderness for her and said to the Muslims, “If you see fit to release her captive for her and return her necklace to her,” (1) and they did so.

(1) Transmitted by Abu Dawud in al-Sunan, 2/69, Book of Jihad, chapter “Concerning the Ransom of a Captive,” hadith no. 2692; Ahmad in al-Musnad, 6/276; and al-Hakim in al-Mustadrak, 3/236, 324. He said, “This is a sound hadith according to Muslim’s criterion, although neither of the two transmitted it,” and al-Dhahabi concurred with him.

Ibn Ishaq related his story at greater length than this: Abu al-As attended Badr with the polytheists and was captured among those who were captured. Zaynab ransomed him, and the Messenger of God ﷺ stipulated that he send her to Medina, which he did. He later came with a caravan belonging to Quraysh; the Muslims captured him and took what he had. Zaynab granted him protection, so he returned to Mecca, delivered the deposits to their owners, and then emigrated to Medina as a Muslim. The Prophet ﷺ restored his daughter to him. The two accounts can be reconciled.

Ibn Ishaq stated that the man who captured him on the day of Badr was Abd Allah ibn Jubayr ibn al-Numan. Al-Waqidi related that the man who captured him was Khirash ibn al-Simma. He said: His brother Amr ibn al-Rabi came with his ransom. Musa ibn Uqba stated that the man who captured him—meaning on the second occasion—was Abu Basir al-Thaqafi, together with the Muslims who were with him when they took up residence [218] on the coast and waylaid the merchants of Quraysh during the truce between al-Hudaybiyya and the Conquest.

Ibn al-Muqri stated in his Fawaid, through Ibrahim ibn Sad, from Salih ibn Kaysan—I reckon, from al-Zuhri—who said: Abu al-As ibn al-Rabi was the man in whose case the grant of protection arose, in connection with the caravan of Quraysh whose members were with Abu Jandal ibn Suhayl and Abu Basir ibn Utba ibn Usayd. He was brought as a captive, and the Messenger of God ﷺ said, “Zaynab has granted Abu al-As protection regarding his wealth and merchandise.” He went out and delivered to them everything that belonged to them. She had asked Abu al-As for permission to leave for Medina, and he had given her permission. He then departed for Syria. When she set out, Hisham ibn al-Aswad and those who followed him pursued her and returned her to her house. The Messenger of God ﷺ then sent someone to carry her to Medina. Abu al-As subsequently joined her in Medina shortly before the Conquest. He said: He traveled with Ali to Yemen, and Ali appointed him his deputy over Yemen when he returned. Abu al-As was then with Ali on the day allegiance was pledged to Abu Bakr.

Abu Ahmad al-Hakim related that he accepted Islam five months before al-Hudaybiyya and then returned to Mecca.

Ibn Sad added that he did not attend any campaign with the Prophet ﷺ.

Al-Bayhaqi transmitted with a strong chain from Abd Allah al-Bahiyy, from Zaynab, who said: I said to the Prophet ﷺ, “If Abu al-As is reckoned close, he is a cousin; and if he is reckoned remote, he is the father of my child. I have granted him protection.” He said: It was also related from al-Bahiyy that Zaynab said this—and that version is mursal.

Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, and Ibn Majah transmitted, through Dawud ibn al-Husayn, from Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas, that the Prophet ﷺ restored his daughter Zaynab to Abu al-As under the original marriage contract. (1) It appears to have been derived from the aforementioned story. Al-Tirmidhi said concerning the hadith of Ibn Abbas: “There is no fault in its chain, but its basis is not known.” He said: “I heard Abd ibn Humayd say: I heard Yazid ibn Harun say ...” He mentioned these two hadiths and said, “The hadith of Ibn Abbas has the better chain, while established practice follows the hadith of Amr ibn Shuayb.”

Al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah transmitted, through Hajjaj ibn Artaa, from Amr ibn Shuayb, from his father, from his grandfather, that the Prophet ﷺ restored Zaynab to Abu al-As with a new dower.

It is established in the two Sahihs, in the hadith of al-Miswar ibn Makhrama, that the Prophet ﷺ delivered an address in which he mentioned Abu al-As ibn al-Rabi and praised him for his conduct as a son-in-law. He said, “He spoke to me and told me the truth; he made me a promise and fulfilled it to me.”

Al-Waqidi said: The Messenger of God ﷺ used to say, “We never found fault with Abu al-As as a son-in-law.” (2)

It is stated in the two Sahihs that the Prophet ﷺ would pray while carrying Umama, the daughter of his daughter Zaynab by Abu al-As ibn al-Rabi.

Al-Hakim Abu Ahmad transmitted with a sound chain from Qatada that Ali married this Umama after the death of her maternal aunt Fatima.

Ibn Manda said: Ibn Abbas and Abd Allah ibn Amr transmitted reports from him.

Ibrahim ibn al-Mundhir said: Abu al-As ibn al-Rabi died during the caliphate of Abu Bakr, in Dhu al-Hijja of the year twelve after the Hijra. Ibn Sad and Ibn Ishaq likewise dated his death to that year and stated that he appointed al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam as his executor. More than one other authority dated it likewise. Abu Ubayd departed anomalously from them and said that he died in the year thirteen. Stranger still is Ibn Manda’s statement that he was killed on the day of al-Yamama.

(1) Transmitted by Abu Dawud, 1/680, Book of Divorce, chapter “Until What Point Is His Wife Restored to Him If He Accepts Islam after Her?” hadith no. 2240.

(2) Transmitted by Ibn Sad in al-Tabaqat al-Kubra, 8/21.

#### English

Humayr ibn Mu'ays ibn Amir ibn Lu'ayy, the Qurashi of the Banu Amir.

His mother, Umm Maktum, was Atika bint Abd Allah ibn Ankatha—the latter name beginning with an undotted ayn, followed by a vowelless nun, and with a three-dotted tha after the kaf—ibn A'idh ibn Makhzum. He was Khadijah, Mother of the Believers', maternal cousin, for Khadijah's mother, whose name was Fatima, was the sister of Qays ibn Za'ida. He embraced Islam early in Mecca and was among the earliest emigrants. He arrived in Medina before the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) emigrated. It is also said that he arrived after him, shortly after the Battle of Badr; this was stated by al-Waqidi.

The first view is sounder. It has been narrated by way of Abu Ishaq from al-Bara, who said: “The first emigrant to come to us was Mus'ab ibn Umayr; then Ibn Umm Maktum arrived.” The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) used to appoint him as his deputy over Medina during most of his military expeditions, and he would lead the people in prayer.

Al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar said: “He went out to al-Qadisiyya, participated in the fighting, and was martyred there. He was carrying the standard at the time.” It is also said that he returned to Medina after al-Qadisiyya and died there; al-Baghawi mentioned this.

Al-Waqidi said instead: “He participated in it, returned to Medina, and died there. No mention of him was heard after Umar ibn al-Khattab.”

He narrated from the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him), and his hadith appears in the Sunan collections.

Abd Allah ibn Shaddad ibn al-Had, Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Layla, Abu Razin al-Asadi, and others narrated from him.

Ibn Abd al-Barr said: “A group of scholars of genealogy and the military campaigns related that the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) appointed Ibn Umm Maktum as his deputy thirteen times: at al-Abwa, Buwat, Dhu al-Ashira, his expedition in pursuit of Kurz ibn Jabir, the expedition of al-Sawiq, Ghatafan, the expedition of Uhud, Hamra al-Asad, Najran, Dhat al-Riqa, when he departed for the Farewell Pilgrimage, and when he departed for Badr. He then appointed Abu Lubaba instead when he sent Ibn Umm Maktum back from the road.” He said: “As for Qatada's narration from Anas that the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) appointed Ibn Umm Maktum as his deputy, the number that reached him did not reach the number reported by others.” End quote.

7794—Muhammad ibn Sayfi [(2)]

Ibn Umayya ibn Abid ibn Abd Allah ibn Umar ibn Makhzum.

Ibn al-Qaddah said: “He had Companionship.” Ibn Shahin mentioned him on the authority of [(3)] Abu Dawud. Abu Umar said: “He did not see the Prophet,” [(4)] and his status as a Companion is open to question. He was Khadijah bint Khuwaylid's grandson through her daughter. I say: Al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar mentioned something that strengthens Ibn al-Qaddah's position. When he mentioned Muhammad's father, he said: “He had [a son named] Rifa'a, after whom he took his kunya, and Sayfi ibn Umayya was killed on the day of Badr.” End quote.

A man whose father was killed at Badr—which occurred in the second year after the Emigration—would have lived for eight years or more during the Prophetic period. He would not have been named Muhammad unless his father and mother had embraced Islam. Perhaps, then, he was born after his father was killed, and his mother embraced Islam and named him Muhammad—or one of his relatives did so, if his mother died before he was named.

بن «١» أبي هالة، ولد الّذي قبله. وعلي قول قتادة ومن تبعه يكون هند بن أبي هند ثلاثة في نسق.
ذكره ابن مندة، وأورد من طريق حسان بن عبد اللَّه الواسطيّ، عن السّري بن يحيى، عن مالك بن دينار، حدثني هند بن خديجة زوج النبيّ ﵌، قال: مرّ النبيّ ﵌ بالحكم أبي مروان، فجعل يغمز النبيّ ﵌ ويشير بإصبعه، حتى التفت إليه النبيّ ﵌، فقال: «اللَّهمّ اجعل له وزغا»، يعني ارتعاشا، قال: فرجف مكانه.
وهكذا أخرجه ابن أبي حاتم الرّازيّ، وعبد اللَّه بن أحمد في زيادات الزهد من هذا الوجه، ومالك بن دينار لم يدرك هند بن أبي هالة، وإنما أدرك ابنه، فكأنه نسبه لجدّه.
وقد ذكر ابن أبي حاتم، عن أبيه- أنّ رواية هند بن هند عن النبيّ ﵌ مرسلة. وجرى أبو عمر على ظاهره، فذكر هذا الحديث لهند بن أبي هالة.
وأخرج الزّبير بن بكّار والدّولابيّ، من طريق محمد بن الحجاج، عن رجل من بني تميم، قال: رأيت هند بن هند بن أبي هالة وعليه حلة خضراء، فمات في الطّاعون، فخرجوا به بين أربعة لشغل الناس بموتاهم، فصاحت امرأة: وا هند بن هنداه! وابن ربيب رسول اللَّه ﵌! قال: فازدحم الناس على جنازته وتركوا موتاهم.

#### English

9028—Hind ibn Hind

Ibn [(1)] Abi Hala, the son of the subject of the preceding entry. According to Qatada and those who followed him, there would thus be three successive generations bearing the name Hind.

Ibn Manda mentioned him and cited, by way of Hassan ibn Abd Allah al-Wasiti, from al-Sari ibn Yahya, from Malik ibn Dinar: “Hind, son of Khadijah, the wife of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him), related to me, saying: The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) passed by al-Hakam, the father of Marwan. Al-Hakam began making mocking gestures at the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) and pointing with his finger, until the Prophet turned toward him and said: ‘O God, cause him to tremble’—meaning a bodily tremor. He immediately began trembling where he stood.”

Ibn Abi Hatim al-Razi likewise transmitted it by this route, as did Abd Allah ibn Ahmad in Ziyadat al-Zuhd. Malik ibn Dinar did not meet Hind ibn Abi Hala; he met only his son. It is therefore as though he attributed the son to his grandfather.

Ibn Abi Hatim related from his father that Hind ibn Hind's narration from the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) is mursal. Abu Umar followed the wording's apparent sense and assigned this hadith to Hind ibn Abi Hala.

Al-Zubayr ibn Bakkar and al-Dulabi transmitted, by way of Muhammad ibn al-Hajjaj, from a man of the Banu Tamim, who said: “I saw Hind ibn Hind ibn Abi Hala wearing a green outfit. He died during the plague, and because the people were occupied with their own dead, four men carried him out. A woman cried: ‘Alas for Hind ibn Hind! Alas for the son of the Messenger of God's stepson!’ The people then crowded around his funeral procession and left their own dead.”
